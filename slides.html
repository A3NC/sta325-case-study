How Turbulence, Gravity, and Inertia Shape Particle Clusters

STA 325 Case Study

Sherry He, Megan Kolenski, Ian Le, Aaron Siminou

The problem and our goals

Our collaborators simulated particles moving through turbulent flow and summarized the distribution of particle cluster volumes in each of 89 runs. Each run used one setting of three parameters:

  • Reynolds number (Re) measures how strong the turbulence is (90, 224, 398).
  • Froude number (Fr) measures the strength of gravity, where smaller values mean stronger gravity and \(\infty\) means no gravity (0.052, 0.3, \(\infty\)).
  • Stokes number (St) measures particle inertia, from particles that follow the flow closely to heavier particles that respond slowly (0.05 to 3).

We had two goals: to predict the mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of cluster volume at a new setting, and to explain how each parameter affects those four statistics.

How we prepared the data

We computed the four statistics from the raw moments in the data. All four are right-skewed and span several orders of magnitude, with kurtosis ranging from about 151 to 132,137, so we modeled the log of each statistic. The cubic model uses linear, squared, and cubed terms in log(St) to capture curvature.

Re and Fr each take only three values, so we treated them as categorical. Every hold-out run uses observed Re and Fr levels; we make no prediction for the missing Re = 398, Fr = 0.3 setting.

The training data cover eight of the nine possible (Re, Fr) settings.

How we chose the model

We compared four linear models and a GAM using leave-one-out cross-validation.

The cubic model had the lowest LOOCV error for all four statistics. It keeps the Re-by-Fr interaction and adds a curved relationship with St; the more flexible GAM had higher error for each response.

Checking the fit

In the baseline, whole groups of runs sit above or below zero for skewness and kurtosis, which shows that the effect of Re depends on Fr. The cubic model captures this interaction and the curvature in St, with \(R^2\) between 0.974 and 0.999. Residuals are centered around zero, though tail departures from normality remain and some spread remains for standard deviation.

Observed and predicted values

The dashed line indicates perfect agreement. Re levels are represented by rows, Fr levels are represented by marker shapes, and St by color. Predictions use all three parameters, including the square and cubic terms of log(St).

Key findings

  • Turbulence controls cluster size. Under strong gravity, raising Re from 90 to 398 makes the mean cluster volume about 410 times smaller and standard deviation about 340 times smaller. With no gravity, the reductions are about 250 times for the mean and 9.6 times for standard deviation.
  • Strong gravity creates heavy tails, but only when turbulence is weak. At Re = 90, strong gravity raises kurtosis about 244 times compared with no gravity. The effect drops to about 9 times at Re = 224 and is small at Re = 398, as turbulence overwhelms gravitational settling.
  • Moderate gravity behaves much like no gravity. In the cubic model, fitted effects for Fr = 0.3 and Fr = \(\infty\) are similar across the four responses.
  • The St relationship is curved. The change in each statistic depends on where St lies in its range, so one doubling percentage does not summarize the fitted effect. The model uses a shared St curve across Re and Fr settings, with different response levels by setting.

Predictions and limitations

For the 23 hold-out runs, we report 95% prediction intervals computed on the log scale. The upper bound is typically about 1.4 times the lower bound for the mean, 2.2 times for skewness, 4.1 times for kurtosis, and 3.8 times for standard deviation. The mean is predicted most precisely.

The main limitations are the small design, with only three levels of Re and Fr and no runs at Re = 398 with Fr = 0.3, remaining residual departures from normality and spread, and possible correlation among runs from the same simulation. Predictions at St = 0.05 are at the observed range boundary. New simulations at intermediate Re and Fr values and at the missing setting would address these gaps most directly.
