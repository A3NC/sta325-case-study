STA 325 Case Study

Sherry He, Megan Kolenski, Ian Le, Aaron Siminou

Problem and Goals

Our collaborators simulated particles moving through turbulent flow and summarized the distribution of particle cluster volumes in each of 89 runs. Each run used one setting of three parameters:

  • Reynolds number (Re) measures how strong the turbulence is (90, 224, 398).
  • Froude number (Fr) measures the strength of gravity, where smaller values mean stronger gravity and ∞ means no gravity (0.052, 0.3, ∞).
  • Stokes number (St) measures particle inertia, from particles that follow the flow closely to heavier particles that respond slowly (0.05 to 3).

We had two goals: to predict the mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of cluster volume at a new setting, and to explain how each parameter affects those four statistics.

Data Preparation

We computed the four statistics from the raw moments in the data. All four are right-skewed and span several orders of magnitude, with kurtosis ranging from about 151 to 132,137, so we modeled the log of each statistic and used log(St) as a predictor.

Re and Fr each take only three values, so we treated them as categorical. A smooth curve through three points cannot be checked against the data, and every hold-out run uses one of the settings we trained on.

The data come from eight (Re, Fr) settings. The combination Re = 398 with Fr = 0.3 was never simulated, so our model has no term for it.

Model Selection

We compared four linear models and a GAM using leave-one-out cross-validation, which refits each model 89 times with one run held out and measures the prediction error on that run.

Adding an interaction between Re and Fr (Model 2) gave the largest improvement over the baseline. Letting the effect of St change across settings (Model 1 and the GAM) made predictions worse, while letting St follow one shared curve (the cubic model) made them better. The cubic model had the lowest error for all four statistics.

Checking Fit

In the baseline, whole groups of runs sit above or below zero for skewness and kurtosis, which shows that the effect of Re depends on Fr. The cubic model removes these patterns, with R² between 0.974 and 0.999. Its residuals are centered around zero, although the tails are heavier than normal and some uneven spread remains for standard deviation.

Lines show the cubic model and points show the observed runs. Every setting shares the same curve in St, shifted up or down by Re and Fr.

Key Findings

  • Turbulence controls cluster size. Under strong gravity, raising Re from 90 to 398 makes the mean cluster volume about 410 times smaller and the standard deviation about 340 times smaller, which fits the idea that stronger turbulence breaks particles into smaller clusters.
  • Strong gravity creates heavy tails, but only when turbulence is weak. At Re = 90, strong gravity raises kurtosis about 240 times compared with no gravity. The effect drops to about 9 times at Re = 224 and almost disappears at Re = 398, as turbulence overwhelms gravitational settling.
  • Moderate gravity behaves like no gravity. The fitted effects for Fr = 0.3 and Fr = ∞ are close for all four statistics.
  • Particle inertia has a curved effect. The standard deviation rises about 9 times as St goes from 0.05 to 0.5 and levels off after that, while skewness and kurtosis peak near St = 0.2.

Predictions and Limitations

Predictions

  • We predicted all four statistics for the 23 hold-out runs, with 95% prediction intervals computed as well.

Limitations

  • The data set is small, at only 89 observations.
  • The data set is “sparse”, with Re and Fr having only 3 levels and no runs at (Re = 398, Fr = 0.3).
  • Runs from the same simulation may be correlated, which might violate the independence assumption.
  • New simulations at intermediate Re and Fr values and at the missing setting would address these gaps most directly.
